    Woodward Weekend and the Brakes I Thought I Knew

    Published Aug 15, 2026 • Updated Aug 17, 2026 • BOBKRANSON

    While the classics rumbled down Woodward, we spent Dream Cruise weekend in a driveway learning that a modern brake job requires a service-mode setting, a BFH, and a good friend with tools.

    #whimsy#lessons-learned#hardware

    It is Woodward Dream Cruise weekend here in the Detroit area, which means forty thousand classic cars are burbling up and down the avenue, and every one of them has brakes a reasonably confident person could service with a wrench, a jack, and an opinion.

    I did not spend the weekend on Woodward. I spent it in a good friend’s driveway, helping my son do a brake job on his car, and discovering — one humbling step at a time — that somewhere between the cars I grew up on and the car in front of me, the entire subject of brakes had been quietly rewritten without anyone sending me the memo.

    Step One: Ask the Computer for Permission

    Here is the first thing nobody told me. Before you touch a modern brake caliper, the brake system needs to be put into service mode — the electronic brakes have to be politely asked to stand down so the humans can have a turn.

    On every car I ever wrenched on, the pre-job ritual was: park it, chock it, crack the lugs. So we came prepared for a whole ceremony. My friend, gratefully, had an OBD connector on hand so we could plug in under the dash and check on the brake system properly. And then came the plot twist: the actual service setting turned out to be sitting right in the normal consumer menus — tucked into the vehicle settings on the dash screen, a few taps away, no cable required.

    I used to know how brakes worked. Apparently now I need to know where the brakes live in the settings menu.

    Step One and a Half: Do the Homework, Because Every Car Is Different

    This one earns its own section, because it is the most genuinely useful thing we learned all weekend: brake service settings vary across manufacturers, and sometimes across models and model years of the same badge.

    Some cars want a scan tool plugged into the OBD port. Some hide a service mode in the infotainment or vehicle-settings menus, like ours did. Some want a particular sequence of ignition switches and pedal presses, like a cheat code from an old video game. And some older or simpler setups still don’t care at all and will let you get straight to wrenching.

    And the stakes are real. The story we got is that if you skip service mode and start compressing things anyway, you can damage the caliper assembly or other parts of the brake system — which is a fancy way of saying you set out to replace some pads and end up buying a caliper. The job you’re doing to save money gets more expensive the moment you skip the free step.

    So before you crack a single lug nut: check your owner’s manual, and go poke through the vehicle-settings menus to see whether a brake service mode is offered right there in the car. Ten minutes of homework up front beats an afternoon of wondering why the caliper won’t cooperate — and a repair bill with extra digits on it. Do the homework first. Then get dirty.

    Step Two: The Precision Instrument Known as the BFH

    Once the computer had granted us an audience, we got to the part of the job that has not changed since the pharaohs: the rotor would not come off the hub. Years of Michigan road seasoning had welded them together like an old married couple.

    The special tool for this situation has a name known to every mechanic on Earth: the BFH. The B stands for Big. The H stands for Hammer. The F stands for the word you say while swinging it.

    There is something deeply comforting about the BFH era surviving into the computer era. The front half of this brake job was software. The back half was percussion. My son got to experience the full spectrum of automotive repair in a single afternoon: from “please wait, connecting to vehicle” to hitting a rusted disc with a hammer the way our ancestors intended.

    Step Three: The Mystery of the Missing Set Screw

    Then came the plot twist. The first wheel came apart agreeably enough — a few good BFH persuasions and the rotor surrendered. The second wheel would not budge. Not for the hammer. Not for the hammer and the word the F stands for.

    The reason, we eventually discovered, is that modern rotors are held to the hub by a little set screw retainer — a tiny screw whose whole job is to keep the rotor located while the wheel is off. The first wheel was missing its screw entirely, which is why it came off so easily. The second wheel still had its screw dutifully doing its job, invisibly defeating the mightiest hammer in the garage.

    Now, this car was bought with only ten thousand miles on it, which means somebody, somewhere, at some point, took that first wheel apart and sent the little screw off to live on a farm upstate. Not a big deal, as it turns out. But it does mean the previous mechanic and I now share a bond: we have both been baffled by hardware smaller than a Cheerio.

    Lesson learned: when the BFH fails, the problem is never the BFH. The problem is a screw you didn’t know existed.

    Step Four: The Wisdom of the Guy With the Tools

    Every driveway brake job has a patron saint, and ours was my buddy — friend, coach, and owner of a garage that contains every tool you could ever need, filed under a system I can only describe as archaeological. His garage is a series of car projects in various phases, and every tool lives wherever it was last useful. Finding the one you need is not a matter of checking a pegboard. It is a matter of asking: which car was this tool last near? Is it in a case, or has it graduated to a drawer? Or is it in that box, with that same bolt, from that other thing? He always found it. That’s the part that matters. The man runs a filing system with no index and a one hundred percent retrieval rate.

    He is the one who taught us the finishing touches that the parts store receipt never mentions: a little grease on the slider channels where the caliper hardware rides, so everything glides instead of grinds. A layer of no-squeak goop on the backs of the new pads, so the neighborhood is not treated to a screeching announcement every time my son slows for a stop sign. Small dabs of know-how, passed hand to hand, the way this stuff has always actually been transmitted — not through manuals, but through a friend leaning over your shoulder saying “here, before you put that back on…”

    What Actually Changed (As Far As I Can Tell)

    By my count, I can tie exactly two things on this job to genuine technological progress:

    • The pads themselves. The brakes I grew up with were asbestos, which stopped the car wonderfully and did terrible things to everyone downwind of the brake dust. Today’s ceramic pads stop better, last longer, and do not require a hazmat conversation. This is progress with no asterisk.
    • The computer’s blessing. Compressing the calipers to fit the new pads used to be a C-clamp and a grunt. Now the calipers have opinions, and the pre-job service-mode ceremony exists so those opinions don’t ruin your afternoon. This is progress with a settings-menu-shaped asterisk.

    Everything else — the seized rotor, the mystery screw, the grease in the right places, the hammer — turns out to be eternal.

    The Part YouTube Cuts Out

    My son has logged more hours watching car videos than some mechanics have logged turning wrenches. Race videos, repair videos, project builds — he is fluent in watching. Which is exactly why, for this job, we didn’t queue up a single tutorial. We went on intuition, a little lean-in, and the twin motivators of saving some bucks and not waiting two weeks for a shop appointment. It was time to get dirty instead of clicking the fun stuff.

    And here is what you only find out when you skip the videos and do the thing: all those repair videos he’d watched have a suspiciously smooth cut between “now remove the rotor” and the rotor being removed. That cut is where we lived this weekend. That cut contains the BFH, the missing set screw, the not-missing set screw, three expeditions into the garage to excavate a different tool, and a good deal of free-form language composed live during the disassembly procedures — the kind of vocabulary no manual documents but every mechanic inherits. The frustrating little learnings in the middle are the whole education — and they are precisely the part that ends up on the cutting room floor.

    So here’s to the middle of the job. To the classics cruising Woodward with their honest, computer-free brakes. To sons who want to learn, friends who can find any tool as long as they can remember which project it was last helping with, and hammers whose middle initial we don’t say in front of the grandkids.

    The car stops beautifully now, by the way. We checked. Twice.

    No rotors were harmed in the making of this post. Well. One was persuaded.
