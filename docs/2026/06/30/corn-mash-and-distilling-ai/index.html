    Corn-Mash and Distilling AI

    Published Jun 30, 2026 • Updated Aug 17, 2026 • MIJUG Team

    The first runnings of a moonshine batch are poison - and so were the first runnings of an AI career. A distiller's lesson on getting to the sweet stuff.

    #ai#whimsy#lessons-learned

    Every old still has a ghost standing beside it, and the ghost is usually clutching its stomach.

    The Foreshot, or: Why Grandpa Went Blind for a Weekend

    If you have ever watched someone make moonshine the proper way, you will have noticed something that looks, to a thirsty person, like madness. The first liquid that drips from the still - the part everyone has been waiting hours for - gets poured straight onto the dirt.

    This first cut has a name. Distillers call it the foreshots, or the heads. It comes off the still first because it boils at a lower temperature than the good stuff, and it is rich in methanol, acetone, and a little cocktail of other things that the human body regards as a personal insult. Drink it, and at best you get a headache that feels like a mule’s opinion of you. At worst, well - the old timers didn’t say “blind drunk” by accident.

    The cruel joke of distilling is that the first thing you make is the thing most likely to hurt you. You have to throw away the very part you were most excited about.

    The moonshiner who learns this the easy way is told by a wiser, older moonshiner. The moonshiner who learns it the hard way spends a long night hunched over a bucket, swearing on his mother’s grave that he will read the instructions next time.

    Reader, I was the second kind of moonshiner. Only my still ran on electricity and my corn mash was made of code.

    The Hearts: Where the Sweetness Lives

    Here is the part the foreshots almost make you forget: after you pour away the poison, something wonderful happens.

    The middle of the run - distillers call it the hearts - is clean, bright, and sweet. This is the good liquor. This is what the whole laborious business was for. And there is a real magic to it, the kind that has loosened tongues and lifted moods and turned strangers into friends around a fire for as long as anyone has been fermenting anything. In the right measure and the right company, a good spirit can unlock a person: the shy become warm, the tired become merry, the guarded tell the truth.

    The catch - and there is always a catch with both liquor and machines - is that the magic only ever lived in the hearts. The poison and the medicine come off the same still, from the same mash, on the same afternoon. The only thing separating “this will hurt you” from “this will delight you” is knowing when to change the jar.

    The First Runnings of My AI Batch

    When I first started building with artificial intelligence, I was drinking the foreshots with both hands.

    I don’t mean I did anything foolish. I mean I did everything that, at the time, was the correct and recommended and best-practice way to do it - and most of it has since been poured onto the dirt.

    I hand-stitched prompts together like a quilt, balancing them just so, only for a new model to arrive a few weeks later that wanted to be spoken to completely differently. I built clever scaffolding to work around things the models couldn’t do, and then the models simply… did them, and my scaffolding became an elaborate monument to a problem that no longer existed. I learned a framework on Monday that was the talk of the town, and by the following Monday the town had moved three streets over and the framework was a ghost town with my name still on the lease.

    This was not old-fashioned obsolescence. A carpenter’s tools last him a lifetime. A blacksmith’s anvil outlives the blacksmith. I had walked into an industry where the corn mash refines itself faster than I could refine a single batch - where the recipe changed not over years, but over weeks, sometimes over a long weekend.

    And like the green moonshiner who didn’t know to throw away the heads, I drank some of those early runnings. I got the headache. I got the long night over the bucket. Each leap forward in the industry - each new standard, each better approach - was genuinely wonderful for everyone who came after it, and quietly invalidated a great deal of careful work for those of us who came before. The pain was real. The rework was real. The “I just learned this and now it’s wrong” was real enough to feel in the stomach.

    Old Bad, New Bad, and the Stuff That Worked Until It Didn’t

    Now, if you want to be fair to the liquor - and we should always be fair to the liquor - you have to admit there are several kinds of batch, and the AI world has all of them:

    • Old bad: The early foreshots. Approaches that were poisonous from the start, even if we didn’t know it yet. We were right to pour these out, and the only shame is how long some of us sipped first.
    • New bad: Here is the humbling part - a fresh batch is not automatically a good batch. New does not mean clean. Every new technique arrives with its own heads to cut, its own ways to make you sick if you swallow the whole run uncritically. The latest thing has foreshots too. It always will.
    • Old that worked, then changed: This is the saddest jar on the shelf, and the most important. Some of what I built was genuinely good liquor - clean, useful, sweet in its day. It worked. And then the still itself was replaced beneath it, the standards moved, and good work became outdated work not because it was wrong but because the whole craft took a great leap while it sat on the shelf. There is no poison in this jar. Only the ache of having made something fine, right before the world decided to make it differently.

    The trick I wish someone had pressed into my hands on day one is the same trick the old distillers have always known: keep your eye on the jar, not the dream. Don’t fall in love with the first thing the still gives you. Be ready, always, to change the jar.

    Distilling, Not Hoarding

    So what does a sickened-but-wiser moonshiner do? He does not quit distilling. He learns to make the cut.

    These days I hold my own work more loosely. I assume the foreshots are coming - that a portion of whatever I build today will need to be poured onto the dirt tomorrow, and I try to build in a way that makes pouring it out cheap rather than heartbreaking. I keep the recipe - the hard-won judgement about temperature and timing and when to change the jar - because the recipe survives even when every single batch made under it does not. I made the mistake of confusing the two for far too long. I thought the liquor was the prize. It was always the recipe.

    And I try to remember the whole reason anyone fires up a still in the first place. Not to make poison. Not even to throw poison away. To get, eventually, to the hearts - to that bright, sweet, unlocking middle of the run that can warm a person up and bring out the best in them. AI, at its honest best, is doing the same thing the good liquor always did: loosening what was stiff, warming what was cold, helping people say the thing they couldn’t quite say and do the thing they couldn’t quite reach. The industry, for all its lurching, has spent the last little while doing exactly what a careful distiller does - cutting the heads, chasing the poison out, and trying to leave more of the sweetness in the jar.

    I just wish it hadn’t all happened faster than a batch of corn mash can ferment.

    A Toast, From a Man Who Still Feels Slightly Green

    So here’s to the foreshots - may we always have the sense to pour them out, and the humility to admit how often we didn’t.

    Here’s to the hearts - the sweet middle, in the bottle and in the machine, that made all the rest of it worth doing.

    And here’s to everyone who drank the early batch with me and lived to tell about it. We didn’t ruin our work. We were just a little early to the still, back when nobody had written down which parts were poison yet.

    Raise a glass. Sip the middle. And whatever you do, for the love of your own stomach - change the jar.

    Drink responsibly. Build responsibly. And never, ever trust the first thing that comes off the still.
